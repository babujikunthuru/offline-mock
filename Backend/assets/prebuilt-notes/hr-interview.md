# Interview Preparation
Interview preparation is a structured way to understand a role, practice relevant stories, and communicate evidence clearly. It includes learning about the employer, reviewing the job description, and preparing thoughtful questions. Preparation should improve clarity without making answers sound memorized. For example, a candidate can identify skills requested in a posting and choose a real project that demonstrates those skills with specific actions and outcomes.

# Behavioral Questions
A behavioral question asks about a real past situation to understand how a candidate acted. Interviewers use these questions because concrete examples reveal decision-making, communication, and learning. A strong answer stays focused on the candidate's contribution while giving enough context. For example, when asked about resolving a disagreement, a candidate can describe the issue, explain how they listened and agreed on a plan, and share what changed afterward.

# STAR Framework
STAR is a framework for organizing behavioral answers into Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Situation and Task give brief context, Action explains the candidate's own steps, and Result describes the outcome and learning. The framework is a guide rather than a script, so answers should still sound natural. For example, a candidate can explain a deadline problem, their responsibility, how they reorganized work, and the team's successful delivery.

# Communication and Listening
Communication is the clear exchange of information, and active listening means paying attention, checking understanding, and responding to what another person actually said. Interview answers benefit from a direct opening, relevant detail, and a concise conclusion. Pauses can help organize thoughts instead of rushing. For example, a candidate who is unsure about a question can ask a brief clarifying question before explaining a reasoned approach.

# Strengths and Growth
A strength is a capability that helps a person contribute effectively, while a development area is a skill they are actively improving. Credible answers connect a strength to evidence and describe practical steps for growth without claiming perfection. For example, a candidate can describe how careful testing prevented defects and mention that they are practicing earlier feedback to avoid spending too long polishing a first draft.

# Questions for the Employer
Questions for an employer help a candidate understand the work, expectations, and team. Useful questions are specific, respectful, and connected to the role rather than easily answered by the job posting. They also show that the candidate is evaluating mutual fit. For example, a candidate might ask how the team defines success during the first three months or how engineers share feedback during design reviews.
